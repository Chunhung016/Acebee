Try a short investigation

  • Seed → young root: open the seed and locate the embryo between the two cotyledons. Which part emerges first?
  • Germination: focus on the seed with Cutaway enabled. Follow the hooked hypocotyl as it lifts the cotyledons above the soil.
  • Growing leaves: compare the first opposite pair of simple leaves with later trifoliate leaves (three leaflets per leaf).
  • Flowers → pods: focus on a flower, advance to fruiting, then open the pods. Connect the ovary, fruit, and new seeds.
  • Seed → hook: focus on the seed during germination. Watch the thin hypocotyl form a hook and pull the cotyledons — still wearing the seed coat — out of the soil.
  • Seedling: compare the two narrow, smooth cotyledons with the first lobed true leaves above them.
  • Compound leaves: count the leaflets on one large leaf, including the tiny leaflets between the big pairs.
  • Flowers → fruit: focus on a flower truss, advance to ripe fruit, then slice a tomato open. Find the locules, the jelly and the seeds.

About this organism

The model represents the common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris), a flowering dicot with epigeal germination. It is one example, not a universal plant life cycle: other species retain cotyledons underground or have different roots, flowers, and fruits. Bean pods are fruits; the beans inside them are seeds.

Opening the seed and pods is an exploded teaching view. Transitions are compressed, detail is enlarged for observation, and the stages do not represent equal periods of time. Mature roots extend beyond the small soil sample shown. Pollination is explained but not simulated.

This model represents the tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), a flowering dicot with epigeal germination. Its tiny seed lifts two narrow cotyledons above the soil, often still capped by the seed coat. Tomato leaves are pinnately compound and hairy, the flowers are yellow and buzz-pollinated, and the fruit is a berry with seeds in jelly-filled locules.

Slicing the fruit is a teaching view. Transitions are compressed, fruit and leaves are enlarged for observation, and the stages do not represent equal periods of time. Measurements are approximate classroom estimates, not growth predictions.
